Across all four: use named regional traditions, not claims to represent an entire faith. Sharing a menu with devil/spider cults risks equating living religions with villain factions; a “Historical religious settings” grouping would help. Excluding sacrificial rooms is good, but changing faith must rebuild the layout, not merely swap objects. Avoid sacred images as randomized loot, destructible clutter, or floor decals. These are representation safeguards, not a claim that religious objects can never legitimately be moved.

Christian

1. Risks: Crucifix and Virgin and Child are appropriate for this medieval Latin setting. Give Mary a devotional setting rather than an arbitrary showpiece position. Keep the altar the liturgical focus; a monumental freestanding crucifix should not displace it.

2. Sacred objects: Showing these objects is acceptable. No INRI is a defensible miniature simplification, not a religious requirement; omitting the plaque is better than fake lettering. Crosses, sacred images, and Eucharistic vessels should not become generic treasure or furniture. Processional objects can legitimately move.

3. Accuracy: Remove enclosed confessionals: they belong to the sixteenth century onward. Avoid automatically filling every medieval nave with later-style pews and kneeling desks. A baptismal font does not replace the distinct function of an entrance holy-water stoup. High Gothic fits the stated region reasonably well, but not most European historical settings, early crusading scenes, or Eastern Christianity.

4. Symbols: Latin cross is right. The labyrinth is an excellent optional floor treatment for a French Gothic cathedral, not a universal Christian emblem. Put it prominently in the nave, not as a small magical-looking seal before the altar. Chartres explicitly treats it as a walkable devotional path. [Chartres Cathedral](https://www.cathedrale-chartres.org/en/cathedrale/monument/le-labyrinthe/)

5. Missing: Add an altar cloth and a rood beam/screen option; mount the large crucifix there for an especially convincing English medieval arrangement.

Verdict: OK WITH FIXES.

Buddhist

1. Risks: Shakyamuni’s described gesture is appropriate. Elevate the image respectfully; keep the chorten clear of doorway traffic and allow circumambulation. Prayer wheels need accessible turning space. A purely ornamental lotus can occur underfoot, but avoid turning it into a sacred mandala or adding sacred syllables.

2. Sacred objects: Ornament-only exterior on the prayer wheel is acceptable as a simplified representation with mantras understood to be inside. An actually empty decorative cylinder would lose its religious function. If animated, turn it clockwise. Buddha images, chortens, and ritual implements should not become loot or casual ornaments. [Rubin Museum prayer wheel](https://rubinmuseum.org/collection/sc2010-32a-h/)

3. Accuracy: Tibetan is coherent for this selection, not a default for most Buddhist history. Reference a particular chorten type rather than assembling generic stupa parts. Wheel-and-deer is especially characteristic of roofline decoration; a tabletop version is possible, but not an obligatory altar centerpiece. Replace the generic square “khaden” specification with referenced Tibetan monastic seating rugs or runners.

4. Symbols: Eight-spoked dharma wheel is correct and broadly recognizable. Keep it off walking surfaces. The brocade banner is acceptable; do not present text-free brocade as a substitute for inscribed prayer flags.

5. Missing: Add a paired vajra and handbell on a ritual table; the hanging entrance bell does not substitute for them. A referenced Shakyamuni thangka would also strengthen the interior. [Himalayan Art Resources](https://www.himalayanart.org/search/set.cfm?setID=6655)

Verdict: OK WITH FIXES.

Hindu

1. Risks: Treat Ganesha as a deity in a sanctum, not an elephant mascot. “Worshipped across every tradition” is too absolute; being commonly invoked first does not make him the principal deity of every temple. Keep footwear and through-traffic outside the sanctum; protect offerings and floor designs from routine trampling.

2. Sacred objects: Showing Ganesha is acceptable. Specify all four hands, attributes, and trunk position from one regional historical example. The installed principal murti should not be a movable decor/loot item; distinguish it from legitimate processional images. No text is workable, but Om is itself a sacred written syllable and needs an explicit, accurately drawn exception.

3. Accuracy: Marigolds are wrong for a strict 900–1300 scene: Tagetes comes from the Americas. Use period-appropriate flowers, or label a separate present-day worship variant. “Kolam” is specifically South Indian terminology; choose a regional North Indian floor-art name and pattern. Reference the torana and lamp locally rather than mixing a generic scalloped arch with a samai. Nagara is appropriate regionally, not representative of all Hindu temples. [Kew’s Tagetes record](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/252092-1)

4. Symbols: Om on a respectful wall roundel, never underfoot, is acceptable. Its exact graphic form should be checked against the intended period; the familiar modern form should not automatically be treated as medieval. A lotus floor design is fine when it remains floor decoration, without deity imagery or sacred writing.

5. Missing: The major omission is architectural: an enclosed garbhagriha opening onto a mandapa. A miniature towered pavilion at the end of an open hall does not adequately convey that arrangement. Provide circumambulatory space where the chosen temple type supports it.

Verdict: OK WITH FIXES.

Muslim

1. Risks: Qibla is the direction toward Mecca, not whatever wall faces the entrance. Orient the room accordingly; put the minbar to the worshipper’s right when facing the mihrab. Preserve clear prayer rows and clean, shoe-free space. Aniconism is appropriate here, but should not become a claim that all historical Islamic art forbids figures.

2. Sacred objects: Text-free mihrab and lamp are religiously acceptable simplifications. Historically, however, inscriptions are characteristic of Mamluk enamelled mosque lamps; geometric replacements should be described as adaptations. An empty rehal is entirely sound. A Qur’an is not forbidden to depict or move respectfully, though excluding it avoids mishandling. [Smithsonian mosque lamp](https://asia.si.edu/education/educator-resources/encountering-religion-in-asian-art/explore-by-object/object/mosque-lamp/)

3. Accuracy: Mamluk Cairo is a strong specific choice, not “the” historical Middle East. Reference the minbar’s canopy rather than assuming a bulb dome. Do not default to identical niche prayer rugs throughout the entire period; plain matting or referenced carpets are safer. Surviving characteristic Mamluk carpets chiefly support the later period. [Metropolitan Museum carpet](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/450467)

4. Symbols: Omitting crescent-and-star is sensible for this pack. The rationale is wrong: the combination predates Islam, so “anachronistic for any pre-eighteenth-century build” is false. An eight-pointed interlaced star is good decorative branding, not a universal Islamic religious emblem; avoid presenting it as one. Ordinary geometric floor stars are acceptable. [Historical study of crescent-and-star coinage](https://doi.org/10.24412/2837-0759-2023-195108)

5. Missing: Add an ablution facility in a courtyard or adjoining area, separated from the prayer floor. Make the minbar optional for small prayer spaces.

Verdict: OK WITH FIXES.
